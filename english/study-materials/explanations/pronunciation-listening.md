# Pronunciation and Listening Explanations

American English IPA is used. Brackets such as [ɾ] describe a common spoken realization; reductions vary with speaker, speed, emphasis, and dialect.

### going to → gonna (casual speech; listening recognition)

**规则与语境：** In casual speech, *going to* is often reduced to /ˈɡənə/ before a verb when it expresses a future plan or prediction. Recognize it in listening; use *gonna* mainly in informal speech or dialogue, not formal academic writing. It is not the usual reduction when *going* means physically moving to a place.

1. I'm gonna call her after class. — 我打算下课后给她打电话。
2. It's gonna rain later. — 晚些时候可能要下雨。

### want to → wanna (casual speech; listening recognition)

**规则与语境：** *Want to* commonly reduces to /ˈwɑnə/ (also /ˈwʌnə/) in fast informal speech. The reduction is most natural when *want to* is followed by a verb; it does not normally replace *want a* before a noun. In careful writing, use *want to*.

1. Do you wanna grab lunch? — 你想一起吃午饭吗？
2. I wanna check one detail before we submit. — 提交前我想核实一个细节。

### flap T between vowels in American English

**规则与语境：** In many North American accents, /t/ (and often /d/) becomes a quick alveolar tap [ɾ] when it occurs between vowel sounds and the following syllable is unstressed. It can sound similar to a light /d/ to learners. It is a pronunciation pattern, not a spelling change, and is less likely under emphasis or at a phrase boundary.

1. I got it already. — 我已经拿到了。（*got it* 中的 t 常变成轻触音。）
2. The city is pretty quiet. — 这座城市相当安静。（*city*、*pretty* 中常听到轻触音。）

### consonant-to-vowel linking across word boundaries

**规则与语境：** When a word ends in a consonant and the next word begins with a vowel, speakers often connect them without a pause. The consonant sounds as if it begins the next syllable. This is ordinary fluent speech, not an extra sound.

1. Turn it off. — 把它关掉。（*turn‿it* 连读。）
2. We need an answer today. — 我们今天需要一个答案。（*need‿an* 连读。）

### unstressed function words often reduce toward schwa

**规则与语境：** Short grammar words such as *a, to, can, and,* and *of* often lose stress and reduce toward /ə/ or /ɪ/ in connected speech. They are usually clearer when contrasted, emphasized, or spoken carefully. Listen for the sentence rhythm instead of expecting every word to have its dictionary vowel.

1. I can do it in a minute. — 我一分钟内就能做完。（非重读 *can* 常弱读。）
2. A cup of tea would be nice. — 来杯茶就好了。（非重读 *a*、*of* 常弱读。）

### sentence stress highlights important content words

**规则与语境：** English speech typically gives more prominence to words carrying new or contrasting information, often nouns, main verbs, adjectives, and adverbs. Function words are often shorter and weaker. Stress can shift to signal contrast, correction, or focus.

1. I asked for the **BLUE** folder, not the green one. — 我要的是蓝色文件夹，不是绿色的。
2. She **SENT** the draft yesterday. — 她昨天把草稿发出去了。（重音突出“发了”。）

### link words smoothly within a thought group

**规则与语境：** Within one meaning group, fluent speakers connect words and avoid inserting a pause after every word. A small pause may mark a clause boundary or change in thought. Smooth linking does not mean speaking quickly; keep the stressed words clear.

1. We tested it‿on the server‿and wrote down the results. — 我们在服务器上测试了它，并记下结果。
2. When the meeting ends,‿send me the notes. — 会议结束后，把笔记发给我。

### and → /ən/ or /n/ in casual speech

**规则与语境：** Unstressed *and* often reduces to /ən/ and may lose its vowel to /n/, especially between items in a list or between closely connected words. It is usually pronounced more fully when stressed or contrasted.

1. We need bread ’n’ milk. — 我们需要面包和牛奶。
2. She checked the logs and restarted the service. — 她检查了日志并重启了服务。（*and* 通常弱读。）

### to → /tə/ when unstressed

**规则与语境：** The infinitive marker *to* is commonly /tə/ in unstressed position. It is more likely to be /tuː/ when emphasized, contrasted, or spoken carefully. Do not confuse this reduction with *to* as part of a stressed preposition.

1. I need to finish this tonight. — 我今晚得完成这个。（*to* 弱读为 /tə/。）
2. We decided to test the smaller model. — 我们决定测试较小的模型。

### can → /kən/ when unstressed

**规则与语境：** Affirmative *can* is often reduced to /kən/ when it is not the focus. Negative *can't* usually keeps a stronger vowel, though exact pronunciation varies by accent. Stress can distinguish ability from contrast or emphasis.

1. We can meet after lunch. — 我们午饭后可以见面。（*can* 常弱读。）
2. Yes, I can help with that. — 可以，我能帮这个忙。（强调能力时 *can* 更重。）

### stop T before a consonant or at the end of a thought group

**规则与语境：** In many North American accents, /t/ may be unreleased [t̚] or glottalized before another consonant or at the end of a thought group. The closure may be audible even when no clear burst follows. This is a common realization, not a requirement; careful speech may release the /t/.

1. I kept the last draft. — 我保留了最后一版草稿。（*kept* 末尾的 t 可能不完全释放。）
2. Put the test file back. — 把测试文件放回去。（*test file* 的 t 可能不完全释放。）

### link identical adjacent sounds as one continuous sound

**规则与语境：** When one word ends with the same consonant sound that begins the next, speakers often hold the consonant once across the boundary instead of pronouncing it twice. The words remain separate grammatically, but there is no extra repeated beat.

1. The big game starts at noon. — 那场重要比赛中午开始。（*big game* 中 /ɡ/ 连续衔接。）
2. We need data analysis first. — 我们首先需要做数据分析。（*data analysis* 中相邻的 /ə/ 连续衔接。）

### should / would / could may reduce and lose the final /d/ in fast speech

**规则与语境：** Before a consonant, the final /d/ in *should, would,* or *could* may be weak or absent in rapid speech, especially in casual conversation. It is often retained in careful speech, before a vowel, or when emphasized. Listeners should use context; learners may pronounce the full forms clearly.

1. I would try the smaller one. — 我会试试小一点的那个。（*would try* 中 d 可能弱化。）
2. You should call before you arrive. — 你到之前应该先打电话。（*should call* 中 d 可能不明显。）
